Defense Tech Is Having Its Moment: What Founders Need to Know

By James Morrison • March 8, 2026

After decades of venture capital avoiding defense-related investments, the sector has experienced a dramatic reversal that shows no signs of slowing. In 2025, defense technology startups raised over $18 billion in venture funding, more than triple the amount from just three years prior. Companies like Anduril, Shield AI, and Rebellion Defense have achieved multi-billion dollar valuations, attracting top engineering talent from Silicon Valley's consumer technology giants. The shift reflects both changing geopolitical realities and a fundamental rethinking of how technology companies engage with national security.

Several converging forces explain the defense tech boom. The most obvious is renewed great power competition, with tensions in Eastern Europe, the South China Sea, and elsewhere demonstrating that advanced military capabilities remain essential. Traditional defense contractors, built for a different era, have struggled to deliver cutting-edge software and autonomous systems at the pace modern conflicts demand. This capability gap creates opportunity for startups that can move faster, attract better talent, and apply commercial technology advances to defense applications. The Department of Defense has responded by creating new procurement pathways specifically designed for innovative startups.

The talent equation has shifted dramatically in the sector's favor. A decade ago, many top engineers categorically refused to work on defense projects, viewing them as ethically problematic regardless of application. That consensus has fractured, partly due to changing geopolitical perceptions and partly due to the nature of the work itself. Much of modern defense technology focuses on intelligence, surveillance, reconnaissance, and autonomous systems that reduce risk to human combatants. Companies like Palantir and Anduril have proven that defense work can be intellectually challenging, well-compensated, and mission-driven in ways that resonate with technical talent.

Building in defense tech requires navigating complexities that don't exist in commercial software. Security clearances create hiring friction and limit which employees can work on which projects. Export controls restrict international expansion and even some domestic collaborations. The government procurement process, while improving, remains slower and more bureaucratic than enterprise software sales cycles. Founders must build organizations that can handle classified work environments while maintaining the velocity and culture that attracts top talent. Those who crack this code gain significant competitive advantages, as the barriers to entry are substantial.

The economic model differs substantially from traditional enterprise SaaS. Defense contracts tend to be larger but less predictable, with funding dependent on budget cycles and procurement decisions beyond the startup's control. Revenue recognition can be complex when dealing with milestone-based contracts and government accounting requirements. However, the most successful defense tech companies have found ways to leverage government-funded development into commercial applications, creating dual-use technologies that serve both military and civilian markets. This approach de-risks the business while expanding the total addressable market.

Investors approaching defense tech for the first time face a steep learning curve. Due diligence requires understanding not just technology and business metrics but also procurement dynamics, competitive positioning against legacy contractors, and regulatory compliance. The most successful funds in the space have built specialized teams with government experience and maintain deep relationships within the defense establishment. They understand that traditional SaaS metrics like ARR growth and net retention translate differently when dealing with government customers and long procurement cycles.

For founders considering the defense tech path, the opportunity is real but requires clear-eyed assessment. Success demands genuine technical differentiation, as the government increasingly evaluates startups against commercial alternatives rather than assuming they offer superior technology. It requires patience and capital efficiency during long sales cycles, relationships that provide insight into procurement priorities, and organizational capabilities that most commercial startups never need to develop. Those who can navigate these challenges are building some of the most consequential technology companies of this generation.
